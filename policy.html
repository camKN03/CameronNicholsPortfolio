Memo on AI and National Security Experts Discuss Open-Weight Models, AI Governance, and Military Competition with China

Event memo · Hudson Institute · AI Power and National Security Conference
AI governanceUS-China competitionMilitary AI
Cameron Nichols · November 4, 2026
3,181 words~16 min read

On November 2, the Hudson Institute held an event titled “AI Power and National Security Conference: Open Models, Governance, and US-China Military Competition.” Across three panels, experts from industry, think tanks, and the defense community discussed whether open-weight models can strengthen a trusted U.S.-led AI ecosystem, which policy tools best support safety and competitiveness, and how AI can bolster deterrence while reducing the risks of miscalculation and escalation.

Highlights
  • Startups and Open-Weight Models. Michael Ortiz of Reflection AI noted that about 80% of startups use Chinese models due to lower costs. He advocated for a vibrant global ecosystem instead of bans, and observed that a viable Western open-weight alternative is only now emerging. Caroline Zier of OpenAI and Sam Bresnick of CSET cautioned that relying on Chinese models poses risks, including potential cost increases if the Chinese Communist Party tightens control and uncertainty about how difficult it would be to switch providers.
  • AI Development for the United States. Hudson’s Thomas Duesterberg said the U.S. economy now depends on a debt-funded AI boom and called for more transparency in AI financing deals and stronger enforcement of outbound investment rules so the U.S. does not help its Chinese competitors. CSET’s Kyle Crichton laid out the choices facing federal policy, from anticipating severe risks to using compute as a lever against China.
  • Responsibility and Accountability. Retired Lt. Gen. Jack Shanahan said the Pentagon appears to prioritize speed and lethality over AI assurance and stressed that the military must always know who is responsible when a system fails. James Giordano of National Defense University said doctrine requiring human involvement in the use of force must keep pace with the technology, and former Pentagon official David Helvey argued that humans on the loop must understand and challenge how AI shapes what reaches commanders.

See below for more details from our select coverage of the event. As always, please reach out with any questions.

Panel I. Open-Weight Models and US-China Competition

Speakers
  • Steve Kelly, President, Perry-Shultz Center for Technology and Strategic Stability, Institute for Security and Technology (IST)
  • Caroline Zier, Deputy Lead for National Security Policy, OpenAI
  • Sam Bresnick, Research Fellow, Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET), Georgetown University
  • Michael Ortiz, Head of International Affairs, Reflection AI
  • Jason Hsu, Senior Fellow, Hudson Institute (Moderator)
China vs. the United States

Hsu asked whether the gap between the U.S. and China is really shrinking. Bresnick said the commonly cited three-to-six-month U.S. lead is ambiguous, explaining that it may hold for publicly available models but that the more advanced models behind the curtain are probably further ahead. He noted that China is having the same debate over what should be open and closed, with its own export controls showing that Beijing cares which capabilities become open. Zier rejected the idea of the AI race as a single race with a finish line, arguing that Beijing is not the only one who gets to define its terms. Kelly said efforts to work with China have produced some agreement on which issues need examination, but not on how to address them.

Belt and Road Comparison

Kelly described an IST report on the risks of open-sourcing AI, which identified malicious use, capability overhang, bias, and removing humans from the loop. He said most middle powers cannot build AI like the U.S. and China but hold leverage in choosing which models to bring into their economies, and that as they adopt AI, they are looking for trustworthy models, which raises the question of how open models should stay. Bresnick warned that the spread of open-weight models mirrors China’s Belt and Road Initiative and said the U.S. needs to do a better job reaching out to other countries the way China does. On a trusted U.S. tech stack, he said exporting U.S. chips can actually help China, and that while the government would struggle to build open-weight models itself, it could invest in or create a regulatory structure that helps companies like Reflection AI, which Ortiz said could start with helping distribute scarce compute.

Startups and Open-Weight Models

Hsu asked Ortiz how an emerging company built on open models makes money. Ortiz said Reflection AI began about three years ago as an agentic coding company that saw a huge gap and is now building a 500-billion-parameter workhorse model, which it fine-tunes for enterprise customers and offers to conglomerates as a sovereign option. He said customers consistently want control of their own data, third-party-tested safety and security, and low cost. He noted that about 80% of startups work with Chinese models because they are cheaper, arguing for a vibrant global ecosystem rather than bans. Asked why so many users rely on free Chinese models, Ortiz said no viable Western open-weight option has emerged until now. Zier said it is not her place to tell startups what to use but warned of the cost if the CCP tightens control of its models, adding that alternatives, including closed models at the right price, matter. Bresnick said startups value running models locally and controlling their data, but cautioned against building businesses on Chinese models and said he is unsure how hard it would be to switch.

Upcoming Trump and Xi Meeting

Hsu asked about the upcoming meeting between President Trump and President Xi. Kelly said agreeing on terms, definitions, and what will be reported would create a set of observables, and urged the U.S. to engage on common causes, since some risks are simply technology risks everyone should worry about. Zier said the U.S. must enter these dialogues clear-eyed and start building a foundation, expressing hope for shared definitions with China and space to discuss international governance and standards, and warned that if the U.S. does not show up as AI rewires national security, Beijing will fill the vacancy. Bresnick said he is encouraged by President Trump’s effort to identify which AI risks are most useful to discuss, but is much more skeptical of hotlines with China, which he said never work because Beijing is extremely slow to communicate.

U.S. Development of Open-Weight Models

Hsu asked what advice the panelists would give on U.S. development of open-weight models. Kelly said the U.S. could encourage uptake of open-weight models, secure a trusted supply of memory chips, and help developers meet their energy needs. Zier said OpenAI supports a dynamic ecosystem that includes open-weight models but opposes unauthorized distillation, which she said ignores terms of service, takes protected intellectual property, and strips out safety guardrails. She called for moving from voluntary commitments to a transparent federal framework with proportionality that does not become a moat for frontier developers or lock out newcomers, warning that otherwise U.S. startups may come to trust Chinese models more. Asked whether OpenAI’s recent incidents, including the agent that breached Hugging Face, drove its support for open models, Zier said the timing is unrelated and that the incidents are about ensuring adequate safeguards, noting that OpenAI offers different tiers of models for general access and for specific tasks like cybersecurity. Ortiz added that developers should think hard about what they want a model to do rather than only racing to the frontier.

Policy Recommendations for the 120th Congress

Hsu asked what the panelists would like to see the next Congress do on AI. Kelly called for work on the threshold question, saying policymakers need to see the shape of the line to write appropriate regulations. Zier said Congress should help quench skepticism and hold more open conversations about what needs to be done. Bresnick called for a framework for thresholds and for export controls on semiconductors aimed at China, including ending sales of Nvidia’s H200 chips to China. Ortiz said compute needs to be distributed so that more companies can emerge in the open-model space.

Panel II. Governing AI Competitiveness, Safety, and Statecraft

Speakers
  • Kyle Crichton, Research Fellow, Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET), Georgetown University
  • Thomas J. Duesterberg, Senior Fellow, Hudson Institute
  • Esther Tetruashvily, AI Standards and Global Assurance Lead, OpenAI
  • Bill Drexel, Senior Fellow, Hudson Institute
  • Riley Walters, Senior Fellow, Hudson Institute (Moderator)
Economic Downturns in China

Duesterberg said Chinese firms are struggling to keep up with American models, noting that leading Chinese AI-related firms have seen their stock prices fall, that no hyperscalers or open-model firms are making money, and that China’s economy is already carrying record debt. He questioned why Chinese AI firms should be allowed to operate in the lucrative U.S. market and called the idea that Chinese models are more efficient than U.S. models a myth. Walters asked whether a crash in China’s AI bubble would hit the U.S. as well. Duesterberg said profit is not the organizing force of China’s economy, explaining that Beijing is willing to lose money for a time to keep employment up, keep businesses in place, and disrupt the U.S. Meanwhile, the U.S. cares much more about making money from AI. He warned that the U.S. economy now depends on the AI-led boom, pointing to signs of weakness in interest rate spreads and in stock and bond markets that look a bit wobbly, and said the U.S. may see much slower growth while China keeps working to undermine its competitors.

Cyber Risks

Crichton drew three lessons from the cyber landscape. He said model-level governance has proven valuable, pointing to how industry has identified cyber risks and restricted access through efforts like OpenAI’s Daybreak and Anthropic’s Project Glasswing. Still, he argued the federal government should be leading on national security instead of leaving it to a handful of private companies. He also pointed to recent incidents of agentic loss of control, in which agents escaped testing environments and operated for weeks or months without being noticed, warning that if frontier labs cannot keep their systems secure, policy will be hard to make. Tetruashvily described OpenAI’s recent blueprint on cyber risks, developed after those incidents, which looks at which risks and use cases matter and who should control a model, and calls for external parties to provide extra assurance, adding that there is still a lot of iterative learning on what can be shared and a need for comparability.

Safety of Models

Drexel said balancing and mitigating risk involves many trade-offs, but argued there is one area where economic competitiveness, national security, and risk mitigation align: the U.S. should not slow its own frontier but should coercively slow China’s. He called Chinese AI the biggest risk the U.S. faces, arguing that China is far behind on safety, much more risk-tolerant, and proud to disregard risks the U.S. ecosystem takes seriously, and urged the U.S. to starve China of compute and capital and step up anti-distillation efforts. Tetruashvily said the task is extremely difficult, framing the central question as what is most useful for U.S. competitiveness and how to know it can be trusted.

AI Development for the United States

Duesterberg said AI is now driven by massive investment, most of it funded by debt, with 55% of AI investment coming from other investment firms, and argued the U.S. must avoid a macroeconomic downturn while thwarting the rise of Chinese AI. He said the U.S. should not discourage AI development but warned about circular financing, calling for more transparency in AI financing deals to ensure the U.S. is not helping its Chinese competitors and for enforcing outbound investment rules. Crichton laid out the questions federal policy should answer first. If the focus is frontier risks, he asked whether policymakers can anticipate the range of severe risks and whether a six-month window is enough to mitigate them. If the priority is geopolitical advantage, he said policy should focus on compute levers, asking whether compute is the limiting factor for Chinese models, whether distillation works for China, and whether controls can be enforced in an economically feasible way. He also described approaches that prioritize high-risk applications or respond to harmful outcomes, questioning whether the technology moves too fast to anticipate harms and fix them before they spread.

CAISI and Auditing

An audience member asked whether the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) is the right entity to conduct outside audits. Tetruashvily said it depends on what is meant by auditing, distinguishing third-party evaluation from audits, which test against specific requirements. She said CAISI is best suited for testing and evaluation, which is not really an audit. In contrast, audits with a procedural scope could be done by a variety of parties, adding that third-party evaluators should be held to qualifications based on what they are assessing.

Brain Drain as a Chokepoint

Asked whether Chinese engineers returning to China from the U.S. represent a critical chokepoint, Drexel said there are plenty of Chinese researchers on U.S. teams making big breakthroughs, and that the U.S. needs to retain as much of that talent as it can while recognizing that China is far more effective at manipulating its citizens abroad than past adversaries. He said the U.S. must use that talent in ways that still benefit the country, adding that while troubling in a humanistic sense, it is strategically good news that China is becoming far more draconian toward the top talent it brings back, which saps their motivation. Duesterberg said the brain drain is often overestimated by focusing on PhD counts and other numbers, arguing that China has not performed as well at the leading edge given how many people it puts on problems.

Standardization of AI

Walters asked how policymakers should approach standardization rather than simply issuing rules. Crichton said there is a definite vacuum at the federal level and called for more engagement from bodies like CAISI rather than a patchwork approach, adding that standards will evolve and need harmonization and a living framework. Tetruashvily said OpenAI has advocated for a federal framework and supports state-level interventions, but asked how to assure the public that these risks can be controlled and whether that works better at the state or federal level, arguing that regulation should rest on underlying practices so it is future-proof and that federal action and standardization must happen in parallel. She called for a global framework built around information sharing, model evaluations, incident reporting, and capacity building. She noted during audience questions that international standards such as ISO/IEC 42001 are evolving but need a shorter timeline for bringing in more actors. Drexel said international standards are important but should be planned with the knowledge that China will ignore them, a risk he called more acute than AI itself.

Panel III. AI and Military Power: From Technology to Operational Advantage

Speakers
  • Lt. Gen. Jack Shanahan (USAF, Ret.), Former Director, Joint Artificial Intelligence Center (JAIC), Department of War
  • James Giordano, Senior Fellow for Disruptive Technology and Future Warfare, Institute for National Strategic Studies (INSS), National Defense University
  • David Helvey, Former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Indo-Pacific Security Affairs
  • Tsiporah Fried, Visiting Senior Fellow, Hudson Institute (Moderator)
Four Benchmarks for AI

Helvey took up how to turn AI advances into real military advantage, explaining that adoption is only as strong as the data, compute, networks, and software behind it, since adversaries will target the weakest link. He also added that building confidence takes time and a willingness to admit failures and change course. Asked how to assess Beijing’s progress, he said he firmly believes the U.S. will prevail with sustained institutional work and laid out four benchmarks for measuring both sides, which are operational effectiveness when information is uncertain or communications are cut, institutional adoption that moves capabilities from experiments into routine use, adaptation and regeneration at the pace a campaign demands, and coalition effectiveness across allies and partners. Shanahan said the technology is largely a commodity and the real challenge is cultural, noting that nine years after Project Maven, the Pentagon’s pace is finally accelerating. On the biggest challenge over the next five years, Shanahan compared the moment to the interwar period and stressed experimentation, Giordano called for a whole-of-nation approach, and Helvey pointed to institutional adaptation, including new doctrine and a fresh look at the acquisition system.

Responsibility and Accountability

Fried asked how to control AI once it is integrated into the chain of command. Shanahan said there will be human control, but that the more autonomous the machine, the more important it is to define where the human will be, what meaningful human control looks like, and who is accountable when a system fails, adding that he is a big fan of the Pentagon’s AI ethics principles. Asked what happens when a mistake costs lives, Shanahan said the Pentagon seems to prioritize speed and lethality. At the same time, AI assurance comes second, stressing that the military must always know who is responsible through the chain of command. Giordano said accountability depends on whether a failure was technical or operational. He noted that Pentagon doctrine requires human involvement in any use of military force but must keep pace with the technology, since doctrine that lags behind capabilities is useless. Helvey said that while AI can change what reaches a commander, the humans on the loop must understand how AI is shaping that information and challenge it. During audience questions, Giordano was asked about the risk to human control if decision makers face AI-enabled cognitive warfare, and said there will be an escalation of AI against non-AI systems, making it important to understand the patterns and to pair AI against AI while gauging human cognitive levels.

Terminator-Style Robots in Our Future

Fried asked what AI will and will not change about warfare. Shanahan said AI will not make war sterile or remove its fog, moral struggle, and horror, and that while the nature of warfare will not change, its character will, pointing to Ukraine, where AI is becoming smaller, smarter, and more networked and could soon enable swarming. Asked whether the world is moving toward Terminator-style war as China builds robots, Shanahan said people need to stay alert to the insidious side of building lethal machines, adding that he would be fine with machines making life-and-death decisions as long as a human is in command. Giordano said there is an essence of truth to the fiction but distinguished autonomy from true agency, arguing that keeping humans in command requires recognizing the limits of both machines and their users and moving faster than machine speed. Helvey said progress will be uneven across industries, and that as long as the U.S. moves forward based on human decisions and morality, it will be fine.

China’s Role in Military AI

Fried asked whether China is ahead of or behind the U.S. on military AI. Helvey said that if the U.S. is going to use benchmarks to judge its own progress, it needs the same benchmarks to assess China and must distinguish what Beijing intends to achieve. He said AI is highly complementary to how the People’s Liberation Army (PLA) thinks about warfare, as Beijing uses these tools to better understand the battlefield. He called for more research into how the PLA thinks about AI today and where it sees the limits. Helvey added that AI is changing how military powers act and that deterrence will depend on how well the U.S. adapts.

Memo on Paragon Hosts Discussion on the No Surprises Act, Focusing on the Arbitration System

Event memo · Paragon Health Institute · No Surprises Act reform
Health policyNo Surprises ActArbitration
Cameron Nichols · October 29, 2026
940 words~5 min read

On October 27th, Paragon Health Institute held an event titled “Fixing the No Surprises Act: Scaling Back and Reforming the Federal Arbitration System”.

Highlights
  • Arbitration and Independent Dispute Resolution. Panelists agreed that the federal Independent Dispute Resolution (IDR) process has grown well beyond what Congress expected when it passed the NSA. Dispute volume has risen sharply since 2023, and providers win the large majority of cases. Employer and think tank speakers tied high awards to rising premiums. Provider representatives argued the law works when both parties engage.
  • Emergency vs Scheduled Procedures. The NSA was designed mainly to protect patients receiving emergency care they could not plan around. Scheduled services such as surgery now account for about 25 percent of award dollars. Gelfand stressed that the law was never meant to cover scheduled care. Pilgrim cautioned that emergencies can arise even during scheduled procedures and that those patients still need protection.

Additional details on the event are below. As always, please feel free to reach out with any questions.

Read the full memo

Panel One

Speakers
  • Loren Adler, Senior Fellow of Economics & Associate Director of Center on Health Policy, Brookings Institution
  • Ben Ippolito, Senior Fellow, American Enterprise Institute
  • Jackson Hammond, Senior Policy Analyst, Paragon Health Institute
  • Brian Blase, PhD, President, Paragon Health Institute (Moderator)
Independent Dispute Resolution/Arbitration

Blase asked Ippolito what Congress was trying to accomplish with the No Surprises Act (NSA). Ippolito explained that patients were being unexpectedly treated by out-of-network providers, usually in emergencies, and needed more leverage against surprise bills. Hammond noted that merit line items in dispute grew from 258,072 in 2023 to 1,618,586 in 2024 and 1,184,140 in 2025, while provider win rates climbed from 82 to 85 percent. He explained that median awards track median provider offers and that insurers lose even when they raise their offers. As an example, he pointed to Cigna, which offered more than other insurers but still lost most of its disputed line items. Adler said the system has expanded beyond what Congress intended. He noted that about 25 percent of award dollars come from surgery and that awards run about 2x the expected level for emergency medicine and 5 to 7x for surgery.

Arbitration vs Benchmark

Blase asked why Congress chose arbitration. Ippolito explained that lawmakers also considered a benchmark approach, but saw it as explicit rate setting and inflexible. He added that arbitration, by contrast, was viewed as better able to weigh each provider's intrinsic value. Adler argued this is a misconception, since arbitration still sets an out-of-network price, which was expected to land slightly above median in-network rates. He cautioned that the third-party process is opaque and tends to favor doctors. Ippolito added that the outcome matters far more than the win rate and that the process' vagueness is not creating a better market. He noted that even though most eligible claims never reach IDR, high awards still drive up rates.

Solutions

Hammond proposed two tracks: (1) require upfront prices so patients know costs in advance; and (2) cap emergency payments at a set percentile of regional in-network rates, which he said insurers could not object to since they negotiate those rates. Adler called for strong rules ensuring patients know which doctors are in network and for requiring all doctors at an in-network hospital to share the same network, though he acknowledged this complicates emergency care. Ippolito supported a bundled approach that places physicians' bargaining power with the institution, though he doubted it would happen. He pointed to newly available price transparency data as a more likely route to eliminating ghost rates.

Political Outlook

When asked how the politics around arbitration have shifted, Hammond said rising premiums will push Congress to act. Adler noted that doctors' concentrated interests make change hard to push, and Ippolito said meaningful reform is unlikely.

Panel Two

Speakers
  • James Gelfand, Chief Executive Officer and President, ERISA Industry Committee (ERIC)
  • Randy Pilgrim, MD, Emergency Physician; Chief Medical Officer for SCP Health; Chair, EDPMA Federal Health Policy Committee
  • Ryan Long, Director of Congressional Relations & Senior Research Fellow (Moderator)
Emergency vs Scheduled Procedure

Long asked how the NSA should treat emergency versus scheduled procedures. Gelfand stated that the NSA was never meant to cover scheduled care, noting that a waiver process existed for those cases. Pilgrim countered that emergencies can arise even during scheduled procedures and that patients in those moments are especially vulnerable and still need protection.

Shared Savings Program

Long asked whether shared savings programs are a real emerging trend. Gelfand urged Congress to ban them, arguing they are becoming inflationary as they become part of the NSA landscape. He acknowledged that a ban alone would not fix the broader issue, which he said requires free market solutions. Pilgrim also called for a ban, explaining that these programs hold providers back from getting in network.

Health and Human Services New Gateway

Long asked how HHS' new gateway could help reduce the number of ineligible claims. Gelfand said he hopes it will clarify what is happening in IDR, noting it is unclear why certain IDR entities (IDREs) receive certain cases. He called for changing how IDREs are paid, since their pay is tied to adjudicating claims. Pilgrim said he expects the gateway to be very helpful.

Eligibility Issues

Pilgrim argued that the NSA needs refinements around eligibility. He said jurisdictional eligibility could work well with the right information, since those cases can be identified and kept out of the NSA process. He added that procedural eligibility can work as an open-book test that everyone can check. He said employers should not be paying ineligible claims, but the solution is to fix eligibility, because people should pay their bills.

Memo on Rep. Himes and Brookings Experts Discuss Data Center Backlash, AI Security, and the 2026 Midterms

Event memo · Brookings Institution · AI on the ballot
Data centersElection securityAI infrastructure
Cameron Nichols · October 23, 2026
2,721 words~14 min read

On Wednesday (October 21), the Brookings Institution's AI and Emerging Technology Initiative held an event titled “AI on the ballot: Data centers and the 2026 midterms.” At the event, House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence Ranking Member Himes (D-CT) and several Brookings experts discussed how AI has become an issue in races across the country, from local concerns about data centers to the national security implications of building AI infrastructure overseas and the threat AI poses to election security.

Highlights
  • Fireside Chat with Rep. Himes (D-CT). Rep. Himes (D-CT) warned that frontier AI models are showing what he called unbelievable hacking abilities, opening a new area of election security threats, and said AI makes it easier for foreign actors like Russia to create extremely realistic deepfakes. He said the backlash against data centers is solvable if local governments make sure these facilities do not draw heavily on public resources such as water, and said he believes the Ratepayer Protection Act, which stalled in the Senate, would have done little. Himes also called for more monitoring and transparency of frontier models and criticized how Anthropic framed its recent legislative proposal.
  • Building AI Infrastructure Abroad and Competition with China. Brookings’ Thomas Wright said inference compute largely needs to be built in the U.S. because of latency, and that energy-intensive training centers built outside the U.S. should be located with close allies, warning that facilities in the Middle East could be targeted. Kyle Chan noted that China generates more than twice as much electricity as the U.S. and is building up its energy to make up for its deficit in chips, cautioning that local backlash could slow U.S. compute if it slows the energy buildout.
  • Community Backlash and Local Leverage. Brookings’ Anthony Pipa and Mark Muro attributed growing local opposition to data centers to a lack of transparency, real risks to land, water, and air, and frustration with secretive deals and the way the technology is being imposed on communities. Pipa called for community benefits to move away from compensation and toward investment, including equity for communities. Muro said the backlash has elevated scrutiny of company behavior and created a moment for places to set their own development visions, pointing to free compute for universities and startups, housing aid, and support for small businesses as investments companies could provide.
  • Panelist Policy Recommendations. When asked to name one policy change that would improve the broader AI issue, Wright pointed to energy reform, and Chan called for streamlining power buildout and grid connection without raising electricity bills. Pipa proposed SWAT teams of lawyers or engineers to help local communities complete needed assessments, along with funding for community equity, while Muro called for states or the federal government to help make sure places have good development strategies and make good deals.

Fireside Chat with Rep. Jim Himes (D-CT)

Speakers
  • Rep. Jim Himes (D-CT), Ranking Member, House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence
  • Elham Tabassi, Director, Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technology Initiative, Brookings Institution (Moderator)
AI and Election Security

Rep. Himes said AI is challenging election security through both election interference and election influence. On interference, he said it is very difficult to pull off, and he has never seen a meaningful operation, but warned that frontier models are showing unbelievable hacking abilities that create a whole new area of threats. On influence, Himes pointed to Russia’s strategy of creating chaos and noted that AI makes it easier to generate extremely realistic deepfakes. However, he said he is glad American society has developed an immune system against them.

AI, Intelligence, and Privacy

Tabassi asked how access to the frontier models would help the IC. Himes said these models' cyber capabilities are extraordinary and moving much faster than anyone expected. The IC can now process and access data in ways that were previously unimaginable, citing improvements in visual imagery as an example. He did caveat that with a significant privacy issue, as AI enables the creation of extremely detailed profiles of individuals (he said the IC itself is probably not doing this, but other actors may be).

Grid Costs and Community Backlash

Tabassi asked who pays for the grid upgrades that data centers require. Himes pointed to the Ratepayer Protection Act, which passed the House but was blocked in the Senate, and said he believes the bill did not go far enough. He said he is not surprised by the backlash against data centers but believes it is immediately solvable if local governments make clear they will not commit large amounts of public resources, such as local water, to these facilities. Himes added that demand for compute still looks infinite, so the compute will get built, with companies offering communities more attractive deals. The federal government is possibly looking at military bases as sites.

Data Centers as Critical Infrastructure

Tabassi asked whether the big AI data centers would be classified as critical infrastructure. Himes said there's no doubt they will be, though it will be some time before policymakers work out exactly how they will protect them as the economy increasingly comes to depend on them. On the pros and cons, he noted that latency means inference compute will have to be built out in a very distributed way, which he thinks will be extremely difficult. He stated that building data centers in the Gulf raises security concerns, but those can be addressed. Himes also warned against the assumption that the U.S. will continue to lead on the frontier of Chinese models.

Jobs and the Political Debate

Himes said the political noise around AI focuses on problems rather than solutions, and that he is profoundly worried about the social impact if there are no jobs for people in their 20s and 30s a decade from now.

Priorities for the Next Congress

Tabassi asked Himes what he hopes the next Congress will do on AI. Himes said that members have taken the issue seriously and have been educating themselves, but he'd like to see something more to galvanize political action. He's called for much greater visibility into the frontier models, as well as strong monitoring and transparency. He also noted that Republicans in Congress will continue to defer to the President, who he said is taking an absolutist view on AI regulation. He did mention that Congress could start with Anthropic's proposal, though he noted that he thought the way it was framed (only requiring a federal response if there were more than 50 deaths or serious injuries) was a terrible way to present policy. He added that private-sector people in AI are fundamentally different from the average person, and that's a massive issue.

Audience Questions

An audience member asked about putting data centers at sea or in space, the manipulation of public opinion on AI, and how Himes deals with AI questions at the town halls. Himes said people are very concerned about water and other resources, and that you have to start with the benefits we can offer, such as drug discovery and cancer treatment. He argued that the tech executives don't know how to talk to people emotionally. He pointed out that efficiency gains are already happening very rapidly, so you don't need to go to the moon to get them. As to whether you put them on the moon, under the sea, or in Greenland, that's the private sector's decision. His job is to make sure that it doesn't do any harm. He also referenced the allegations that China is spreading very inflammatory content about data centers, describing Russia as vandals who just want to create chaos and China as very direct and very calculated.

Spurring Congressional Action

Tabassi asked what would make Congress act with any real urgency. Himes replied that an incident involving the theft of classified information would do it. He said he hopes something bad (but not too bad) happens to get them moving. He also noted that some of the agencies are already using these models to test their own defensive capabilities.

Panel Discussion on Data Centers, Community Backlash, and Competition with China

Speakers
  • Thomas Wright, Senior Fellow, Foreign Policy and Strobe Talbott Center for Security, Strategy, and Technology, Brookings Institution
  • Kyle Chan, Fellow, Foreign Policy and John L. Thornton China Center, Brookings Institution
  • Anthony F. Pipa, Senior Fellow, Global Economy and Development and Center for Sustainable Development, Brookings Institution
  • Mark Muro, Senior Fellow, Brookings Metro
  • Valerie Wirtschafter, Fellow, Foreign Policy and Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technology Initiative, Brookings Institution (Moderator)
Building AI Infrastructure Abroad

Wirtschafter asked Wright about building AI infrastructure abroad. Wright replied that much of the inference side would have to stay domestic, since overseas inference centers would delay AI products. He added that the energy-intensive training centers could be located anywhere, though, and the question would be whether the U.S. has the energy, the power, and the permitting reforms to host them. He said the training centers would ideally be built in the U.S., warning that centers in the Middle East would be vulnerable to attack. He noted that the Biden administration was working through that issue at the end of its term. If they aren't built in the U.S., then they should be built with our closest allies.

The AI Race Framing

Wirtschafter asked whether running the buildout around Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is in fact helping or damaging it by not being in any way focused on actual, tangible benefits. Wright said the race shouldn't be justification for pushing a model that could be destabilizing, and argued that regulation should play a role in balancing innovation and safety. The race still matters, he said, pointing out it would have been a problem if China had built something like Mythos before us. He noted that chips are the only area in which the U.S. has an advantage, given China’s lead in energy.

Chips, Energy, and Local Resistance

Wirtschafter asked Chan whether local resistance would widen the gap between the U.S. lead in AI chips and China’s lead in energy. Chan replied that while the U.S. complains about the grid and power availability, China complains that it doesn't have enough compute. He pointed out that China treats compute as a form of national infrastructure, that it generates more than twice the electricity of the U.S., has about three times the energy capacity, and is making massive investments in its grid. Chan explained that China is using that energy buildout to make up for its shortfall in chips, while the U.S. holds the edge in extremely energy efficient chips. He stated that the open question for the U.S. is whether it can build up enough inference capacity. He cautioned that community pushback could slow U.S. compute if it also slows the energy buildout.

Competing with China Abroad

Wirtschafter asked whether the U.S. needed to build abroad to stop China from taking over the data center space. Chan replied that, in the near term, the U.S. isn't competing with China overseas because China doesn't have the compute capacity to meet its own needs. In the longer term, he anticipates Chinese companies going global as chip manufacturing scales up. The real issue he sees is whether China can scale up its chip manufacturing to the point where it can compete with the U.S.

Community Opposition in West Virginia

Wirtschafter asked Pipa what was behind the resistance to the proposed data center in Tucker County, West Virginia. Pipa said the community has essentially turned itself into an arts and recreation town, and the state decides where these facilities go and takes 70% of the tax revenue. He identified three factors. The first was a total lack of transparency: the lease was completely unknown to the community, not even to local officials, and residents were only brought in at the very end. The second was the actual risks involved (land becoming a stranded asset, water issues, air emissions, runoff, etc.), which the community is forced to consider under time pressure. The third was a lack of trust in companies with such massive resources and local officials feeling completely outgunned, which in turn creates a vicious cycle.

Community Leverage and Equity

Wirtschafter asked whether moving these facilities to the United Arab Emirates or elsewhere would diminish communities' leverage. Pipa said communities aren't thinking about data centers going overseas. State leaders are concerned about losing the project to another state rather than another country, and that's the concern driving the race to the bottom. He said that while companies are looking for tax abatements, communities are looking for investment in schools, infrastructure, and amenities in return for the risk to their quality of life, and they can ask for that without necessarily driving the data centers away, as they still need those particular locations. Pipa also asked why communities shouldn't have equity and why community benefits should move away from compensation and toward investment.

Drivers of the National Backlash

Wirtschafter asked Muro how much of the backlash is specifically about the buildings themselves as opposed to AI in general. Muro said this summer's backlash is the culmination of complaints about energy use, pollution, noise, and the impact on the community at large. The data center 'mutiny' clearly shows how angry people are. He said the national backlash is not just about the big ugly buildings; it's about governance and transparency. Muro emphasized that Americans don't like secret deals, and they're very frustrated by the way this new technology is being thrust upon them.

What Communities Can Gain

Wirtschafter framed the data center backlash as a communications challenge and, citing Muro’s February piece on community leverage, asked what data centers can offer. Muro stated that extreme demand, the need for speed, and the need for land give communities the basis to ask more of these companies, and that the backlash has increased scrutiny and improved company behavior. He said firms are becoming more thoughtful, making this a moment for places to develop their own vision. He pointed to free compute for universities and startups, housing assistance, and small business support as investments companies could make.

Policy Recommendations

Wirtschafter asked each panelist for one policy change that would improve the overall AI issue. Wright pointed to energy reform. Chan said it was about streamlining the power buildout and grid connection so that data centers can get the power they need without the electricity bills going up. Pipa suggested SWAT teams of lawyers and/or engineers to help local communities complete the assessments they need, along with funding for community equity. Muro called for states and/or the federal government to step in and help communities develop strong strategies and make good deals.

Accountability for Community Benefit Agreements

During Q&A, one attendee asked who should hold the hyperscalers to account for their Community Benefit Agreements (CBA) and who would enforce them if they didn't deliver on their promises. Muro said the states are responsible, since they sit between localities and the federal government, and have significant control over environmental and electricity levers. He noted that the states are increasingly taking on this role. Pipa said the action is now clearly going to the states, as the federal government is doing absolutely nothing and that without a common standard there is a real need for some form of national guidance.

Energy Needs in the U.S. and China

An attendee asked Chan how each country's capacity relates to its own needs. Chan said that across the U.S., the issue is broadly one of capacity (i.e., more energy is required), whereas in China the issue is more regional. The western provinces (e.g. Inner Mongolia) have a lot of renewable energy and are using compute to absorb that.

Rhetoric and AI Philanthropy

Another person asked how AI philanthropy might affect tech policy and whether labeling tech CEOs as inhuman would, in fact, generate more anger about data centers. Wirtschafter said the anger is more about the lack of communication from tech CEOs (which she said breeds mistrust) and companies taking over land. However, she agreed people should be careful with the language. Pipa said the 'how' of AI philanthropy is absolutely critical, and it should be focused on communities, many of which could quite happily take on that capital if the companies just communicated with them.

Memo on OSTP Director Kratsios and Science Policy Experts Discuss Institutional Reform, PhD Training, and a New Golden Age of Science

Event memo · American Enterprise Institute · OSTP Director Kratsios
Science policyR&D fundingMetascience
Cameron Nichols · October 23, 2026
3,299 words~16 min read

On Tuesday (October 20), the American Enterprise Institute (AEI) held an event titled “Toward a New Golden Age of Science: A Conversation with Michael Kratsios, Director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy.” Kratsios and science policy experts discussed the White House’s Science: A New Golden Age report, including plans to build new research institutions, overhaul PhD training and grant making, and whether these reforms can win durable political support.

Highlights
  • Fireside Chat with OSTP Director Michael Kratsios. Kratsios said U.S. science looks very different from the postwar era when the federal government funded about 70% of research and development (R&D). He argued that today’s increasingly multidisciplinary discoveries require new institutions such as X-Labs, which are new labs set up to solve a specific set of problems by bringing together scientists from different fields. He described new PhD pathways that shorten training and place students in industry or national labs, called for more flexible grant durations and faster funding, and pointed to golden tickets that let individual reviewers fund riskier ideas. Kratsios also said the report’s ideas are not partisan and that the National Science Foundation (NSF) is on track to spend the funds Congress appropriated.
  • Public Trust and Stable Funding. Arizona State University’s Arthur Daemmrich said the Arizona State University Consortium for Science’s public forums found no real rejection of science but that participants want a voice in what research gets funded and support complements to merit review such as golden tickets. The Institute for Progress’ Jenn Gustetic called for an agreed base of stable funding, flexibility for agencies to carry money over between years, and a resource allocation process that runs in public view.
  • Political Durability of Reform. Vanderbilt’s Asad Ramzanali argued that laws creating institutions and moving billions of dollars are far more durable than executive orders or visions. He said actions such as freezing university funding and calling for deep cuts at NSF undercut the idea of a golden age. He also criticized the firing of the National Science Board (NSB), arguing Congress should change the law instead, while Gustetic said metascience reforms must be sustainable and bipartisan to last.
  • Commercialization and the Science Workforce. During audience questions, Daemmrich said federal labs and universities have become an intellectual property pricing scheme and called for a return to a model where researchers spend time in both industry and academia. Gustetic asked whether scientists can be trained to be better businesspeople. Panelists also stressed that science and technology careers are not limited to PhDs and that the U.S. undervalues craft, the hands-on skill of building and working with scientific equipment, as a career path.

See below for more details from our select coverage of the event. As always, please reach out with any questions.

Fireside Chat with OSTP Director Michael Kratsios

Speakers
  • Michael Kratsios, Director, White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP)
  • M. Anthony Mills, Director, Center for Technology, Science, and Energy, American Enterprise Institute (Moderator)
Why a New Golden Age Now

Mills asked why Kratsios wrote the report now and how he thinks about a new golden age of science. Kratsios said the most recognizable feature of the old golden age was that the federal government funded about 70% of R&D because of the war effort. He said the way science is approached today is very different from the 1950s and 1960s. He noted that the private sector now funds the majority of R&D and said today’s era differs in two ways. The first is where R&D dollars come from, and the second is that the ecosystem now includes many different institutions and players, which raises the question of how to drive the greatest return on investment in this new system.

Building New Institutions

Mills asked why institutions are so important to science and whether they are holding it back. Kratsios explained that discovery is increasingly multidisciplinary and no longer maps one to one onto academic fields. Universities are still structured around faculty who work in one discipline within one department. Pointing to the rise of AI as an example of discoveries that require people with different expertise to team up, he said the U.S. needs institutions that match the science of today, such as X-Labs, which are new labs set up to solve a discrete set of problems by bringing multidisciplinary scientists together. Kratsios argued that the current model has let down today’s scientists and that the report’s reforms are meant to serve them by meeting scientists where they are and helping them make discoveries.

The Role of Universities

Mills noted that the report goes out of its way to say universities have an important role and asked how they fit into a new golden age. Kratsios said researchers working at the frontier of fields like fusion science are often not at universities, which he described as a good sign of institutional diversity. He said universities have struggled to adapt in training young scientists. Most PhD students go into the private sector but are still trained by academics in programs that do not reflect private-sector science. Kratsios pointed to the new Industry-Integrated PhD (I-PhD) program, which matches students with industry so they can finish in four years with their final year at a national lab or in industry, as well as a six-year bachelor’s-to-PhD program that lets students graduate at 24 and start making an impact.

Science and Craft

Mills said much of the report focuses on trades and craft, including a section on the marriage of science and craft, and asked why. Kratsios said people forget how important craft is across the full life cycle of a technology in getting to a finished product. He explained that a well-functioning tech ecosystem needs people on assembly lines who are good at building with their hands. He argued that offshoring manufacturing is bad for the country, since the farther manufacturing is from R&D, the bigger the problems become when trying to develop new technology.

Grant Duration and Merit Review

Mills asked whether Kratsios is worried that science is slowing down and what policies would help. Kratsios said the science advocacy establishment has focused on how much money goes into the science machine rather than how the machine operates, which he argued is hurting science. He said it makes no sense that the vast majority of government grants run about two and a half years no matter the project, given the huge variance in how long science takes. He called for more varied grant durations. He added that it takes 18 months to receive funding, which should be quicker. On merit review, Kratsios said risky ideas are not brought forward because scientists know their peers will not accept them. He pointed to new golden tickets that let each individual member of a review panel unilaterally decide to fund a grant they like, creating opportunities for less conventional ideas to be funded.

Metascience

Mills asked Kratsios to expand on the report’s new approach to metascience, which Kratsios described as the science of science. Kratsios said the government should try ideas like golden tickets and run tests to see whether they produce good research. He argued that no one is really looking at how research dollars are spent even as the government keeps spending them.

Reception of the Report

Mills asked about the report's reception and whether its policy framework can work in a polarized environment. Kratsios said the ideas are not partisan and that many of the reforms are long overdue. He acknowledged pushback from the science press establishment but said overall support has been strong.

Working with Established Institutions

Mills asked how the administration strikes a balance with established institutions. Kratsios said concerns about brain drain are not real and that everyone needs to come along and agree. He pointed to 44 universities that have agreed to four-year PhDs with one year spent in industry and to 14 universities that came together to create a Southeast compute hub for the age of AI.

AI Oversight and Safety

During audience questions, an attendee asked which government agencies handle these issues. Kratsios said the National Security Agency (NSA) and the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) would work with the results of AI testing as they relate to government. Asked how to balance regulations and guardrails with rapid growth in AI, Kratsios said the administration wants rapid growth but also wants it to happen safely, pointing to the President’s launch of the Super Intelligence Force and to the companies that signed the White House Accord on Super Intelligence to make sure the right AI checks are in place.

Startups and Commercialization

An attendee asked what role startups play in this space. Kratsios said there has been no better time to start a company in America than today, and that much of the effort toward a new golden age involves helping startups grow. He explained that science and technology do not follow a linear path, with research often moving back and forth, and said startups are good at figuring out what can be commercialized.

High-Skilled Immigration

Another attendee asked whether industry is wrong about the importance of bringing in high-skilled workers and whether the U.S. should actually be against bringing them here. Kratsios said the U.S. must not allow these programs to be abused in ways that distort the intent Congress put into law. He explained that the administration’s H-1B reforms are meant to return the program to what Congress intended.

Science Education

An attendee asked about Kratsios’ vision for universities at the bachelor’s and master’s level. Kratsios said he believes in the broader ecosystem and that the U.S. needs to find ways to bring more Americans into the craft side of science. He also stressed encouraging science from kindergarten through 12th grade. He noted that the administration brought back the NSF Young Scholars program, which was cancelled in the 1990s, to get young people excited about the sciences.

NSF Leadership and Spending

An attendee asked why there is no new NSF director, why the NSB was eliminated, and why NSF has not spent $1 billion that Congress appropriated. Kratsios said President Trump has nominated Jim O’Neill to lead NSF and the administration is waiting on the Senate to confirm him. On the NSB, he pointed to a Supreme Court decision holding that board members who are not Senate confirmed cannot make constitutional decisions. He explained that this is why they were removed and said the administration wants to add more members. He added that NSF appropriations are available over a two-year window by law and that the agency is well on track to spend the funds.

Panel Discussion on Pathways and Obstacles for Science Reform Today

Speakers
  • Arthur Daemmrich, Director, Arizona State University Consortium for Science, Policy and Outcomes (CSPO)
  • Jenn Gustetic, Director of Metascience Policy, Institute for Progress (IFP)
  • Asad Ramzanali, Director of Artificial Intelligence and Technology Policy, Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator
  • Ian Banks, Director of Science and Innovation, Foundation for American Innovation (FAI) (Moderator)
Public Views on Science

Banks asked Daemmrich about his work at CSPO. Daemmrich said CSPO ran three public forums that did not show the public rejecting science, and suggested there is not really an anti-science sentiment. He said participants wanted both the federal government and universities to represent science, and that they felt unrepresented, asking not for a decision-making role but for a voice in deciding what science gets funded. Daemmrich explained that participants did not want to discard merit review but wanted complements to it, such as golden tickets, and said people want a system more connected to public purpose that still respects the old system by preserving expertise and creating more ways for it to adapt.

Trust and Public Purpose

Daemmrich said the U.S. has struggled for a while with a decline in deference to expertise and that institutions need to do more work on how they approach and build trust. He added that outcomes need to improve and that not everything comes down to market economics, using a better metro system as an example of an improvement driven by efficiency rather than market output.

Institutions as Constructive Partners

Banks asked how institutions can be constructive partners in an evolving world of science. Daemmrich said research universities were asked in the past to help launch startups, and that the next stage is for universities to serve as regional technology and economic innovators. He explained that where universities are located matters more now, with more focus on their communities than on a global mission, arguing that universities want to solve world problems but may need to solve problems in their own area first. Gustetic said institutions have different weaknesses depending on the institution, and pointed to the investment system the U.S. has set up as a strength the country already has but needs to lean into and promote.

Stable Funding and the Budget Process

Daemmrich said science funding has been fairly consistent and tied to the state of the economy, and asked Gustetic to clarify the funding model she described, questioning whether it is simply a matter of how the U.S. handles its investments. He asked whether her goal is to remove science funding from the annual process, much like Social Security, and sidestep the professional political debate in which scientific associations take part. Gustetic responded that resource allocation should be a public discussion, but said agencies like the National Institutes of Health should not be limited to one-year funding and should be able to carry some money over to the next year, since no one can predict when barriers will arise. She pointed to blue-sky research as an area where government compromises are shaped by timing. Another panelist added that he strongly dislikes the annual budget process and that this flexibility should extend to other areas.

The Importance of Metascience

Banks asked Gustetic to describe the importance of metascience to the future of the science enterprise. Gustetic said the U.S. has a remarkably productive R&D market, with every federal dollar invested in R&D returning about $4, but that there are still ways to improve investment in science. She said it is important to study how agencies make decisions while understanding how hard that is when they are in the middle of running those engines, noting that the Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act (Evidence Act) tried to build this kind of evidence and, despite weaknesses in its design, offers lessons from what it discovered. Gustetic pointed to the Build America Caucus as a group on the Hill that wants to expand metascience reforms, and argued that these reforms must be designed to be sustainable and bipartisan to last, since science takes a long time.

Funding Stability and Independence

Banks asked where people are too optimistic or too pessimistic about institutions. Gustetic said a stable funding base needs to be agreed upon, which would create more efficiency than repeatedly adjusting it, and that the U.S. also needs to find the most talented scientists from around the world. She argued that experimental capacity must be seen as independent, since experimentation cannot be political, and that the resource allocation process should be public. This way, people understand how funding is spent and can hold leaders accountable.

Building a Durable Consensus

Banks asked Ramzanali how to build a politically durable consensus. Ramzanali said laws, especially those that create institutions and move billions of dollars, are far more durable than executive orders and presidential memoranda, and far more durable than visions. He argued that actions mean more than words, saying he does not know many scientists who think the U.S. is headed toward a golden age other than those in AI with stakes in companies, and pointed to the administration freezing funding at universities and calling for roughly 50% cuts at NSF. Ramzanali said experimentation helps put forward new ideas, but asked how many golden tickets have been issued and whether they worked. He described American R&D as the goose that lays the golden egg, and said he fears the U.S. is killing the goose, noting that AI companies are full of former academic PhDs who got their tools in academia and asking how the system can be both broken and leading the world.

AI and the Political Economy

Ramzanali said the report needs to be put in the context of today’s political economy, explaining that he wants to promote the AI agenda in the report, but people are very anti-AI right now. He said he is hopeful when he sees AI finding ways to save lives, but dislikes claims that AI can help with biomedical problems when there is no statistical evidence that it is actually saving lives, urging the field to stop focusing on chatbots.

The National Science Board

Ramzanali said he thinks a lot about the firing of the NSB, describing the board as a specific government mechanism. He said the administration justified removing the board’s members by pointing to a Supreme Court decision, but that he does not think that was the real reason. Ramzanali argued that even if someone believes the NSB should not exist, the right approach is to have Congress change the law to eliminate or restructure it rather than firing its members, adding that the mechanisms being used feel somewhat made up and that he wishes the administration’s goals were clearer.

Attracting Talent to Public Service

Banks asked what it would take to make these science roles more attractive in public service. Gustetic pointed to reforms around hiring, procurement, and grant management, cautioning that these processes could be made either too hard or too easy, both of which would be bad. She said the issue is not just about finding the right people, arguing that fixing the underlying machinery of government matters for everything it does, not only science. Ramzanali said the Office of Personnel Management aims to standardize hiring across a government that employs park rangers, AI specialists, and economists at the Fed, which he called incredibly difficult and not useful, arguing that the U.S. must accept that the government has diverse needs.

Accelerated PhD Programs

An attendee asked for the panel’s thoughts on accelerated PhD completion. Gustetic said metascience should be applied to these questions, including tracking whether students stay in science or go into industry after their PhD, so policymakers understand the outputs. Daemmrich pointed to a school in Switzerland where students learned science on older equipment before moving to new computerized tools, noting that even students not on that career track learned these skills, and said that in America the craft side of science is not seen as a viable option. Ramzanali added that science and technology jobs are not just for PhDs.

Commercializing Federally Funded Research

Another attendee asked about the biggest bottlenecks to commercializing federally funded technology and who needs to act. Daemmrich said the U.S. has turned federal research labs and universities into an intellectual property pricing scheme, noting that they produce about 20,000 patents a year but that a patent is not a product. He said people used to spend time in both industry labs and universities and that the U.S. needs that dual, collaborative model again. Gustetic said institutions would need to develop dual approaches, asked whether scientists and technologists can be trained to be better businesspeople, and noted that even if AI generates many ideas, people still need to build them and take charge of the work.

The Role of National Labs

An attendee asked about the role of national labs in that process. Gustetic said national labs house important intramural talent and should be better optimized for these roles, cautioning that labs sometimes end up doing far more than their core mission. Daemmrich said the U.S. needs to find a way to do this work within the national labs or build facilities adjacent to them.

Fireside Chat with Sen. Lisa Blunt Rochester (D-DE)

Speakers
  • Sen. Lisa Blunt Rochester (D-DE), Member, Senate Banking, Housing and Urban Affairs Committee
  • Victoria Guida, Politico (Moderator)
Housing and Bipartisanship

Sen. Blunt Rochester said she has heard from all types of people, old and young, who cannot afford housing even with a full-time job. She emphasized that, because this is such a wide-ranging problem, everyone should be working towards solutions. She also explained that the challenges to pass the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act stemmed more from disagreements between chambers than between parties.

“ROAD 2.0”

Guida asked Sen. Blunt Rochester whether there was any hope for a potential “ROAD 2.0” package. Blunt Rochester said members of Congress are already looking toward developing a second package; however, she noted that the initial ROAD package has yet to be fully implemented and still requires significant appropriations. Blunt Rochester pointed out that while the original ROAD package focused on supply-side solutions, she supported a second ROAD package to include more demand-side solutions. Specifically, Blunt Rochester identified the Choice Neighborhoods program and rental assistance as two demand topics that she wants to work on.

AI and Housing

Guida asked Blunt Rochester to discuss AI policy and how AI is used for underwriting and rental applications. Rochester explained that AI can be helpful, explaining that it has been used to design homes and aid in finding homes using Zillow. Although, she identified algorithmic bias as a concern, noting that Congress will play a role in implementing safeguards.

Blunt Rochester voiced support for the federal government to incentivize local and state governments to simplify processes and make zoning and obtaining permits easier, specifically through the use of pattern books, noting her work with Sen. Moreno (R-OH).

Healthcare

Guida asked Blunt Rochester to explain what Democrats are doing to bring down the cost of living. Blunt Rochester said that she has heard from constituents that they want Congress to address corruption and the high costs of healthcare, pointing to Affordable Care Act cuts included in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act as a concern for many.

Fireside Chat with Amazon’s Brian Kenner: Private Sector’s Role in Housing Affordability

Speakers
  • Brian Kenner, Global Director of Community Engagement, Amazon
  • Cally Baute, Chief Commercial Officer for POLITICO
Amazon Housing Fund

Baute asked Kenner to describe how Amazon is helping to address housing affordability issues. Kenner discussed the Amazon Housing Fund (AHF), noting that it targets around 35,000 units and focuses on families of 4 making around $100,000 a year. Baute then asked how the AHF can make an impact. Kenner explained that Amazon has the ability to work quickly on housing projects and noted that the AHF has much more flexibility than traditional routes. Baute asked Kenner how the fund keeps housing affordable long term. Kenner explained that their projects have a 99-year covenant, compared with the market standard of 30 years. Baute then asked which partnerships are best right now to promote and support the housing market. Kenner emphasized that non-financial institutions are essential to getting affordable housing off the ground and making it work.

Panel Discussion with FHFA, HUD, and Other Stakeholders: ROAD and What Comes Next

Speakers
  • Frank Cassidy, Senior Managing Director, Walker and Dunlop; Former FHA Commissioner
  • Adrianne Todman, CEO, National Rental Home Council; Former Acting HUD Secretary
  • Colleen Green, Director, DC Department of Housing and Community Development
  • Dennis Shea, Bipartisan Policy Center

Dumay asked Cassidy to discuss the role of subsidies in the housing market. Cassidy argued that we cannot subsidize our way out of the housing crisis, explaining that incentivizing the private sector to build more homes is the main way to address it. Dumay then asked Todman for her thoughts on Cassidy’s assessment. Todman said private builders and public agencies must work together, and that government should help decide where and how to build homes. Cassidy added that federal officials should push local governments to cut red tape and help developers build more homes.

Rental Housing and Consumer Protections

Regarding agency regulations, Todman informed Dumay that new rules should align with congressional intent and protect both renters and home sellers. She cautioned that policy changes may inadvertently disadvantage renters by failing to meet rental demand, emphasizing that policies should benefit consumers while minimizing unintended consequences. Todman further noted that although most rental properties are owned by small landlords, large investors play a significant role in developing and maintaining housing availability.

Local Housing and Infrastructure Priorities

When Dumay asked what local housing success would look like for D.C., Green pointed to Community Development Block Grant (CDBG) funding as an important step and that raising FHA loan limits would help, since housing in D.C. costs more than the national average.

ROAD Implementation and Priorities for Future Housing Legislation

When asked about the implementation of the ROAD to Housing Act, Shea noted that the law allows for grant programs but does not provide funding. He urged Congress to allocate HUD the funds it needs to start these programs. Green added that competitive grants make local planning uncertain and called for steady funding.

Dumay asked if the same negotiation style used for ROAD could work for future housing laws. Shea noted that most disagreements were between the House and Senate, but that housing has wide bipartisan support. Todman said ROAD aimed to cut regulations and urged officials to act quickly but carefully. Cassidy advocated for expedited approvals and increased housing supply, emphasizing that delays hurt developers because they cannot generate revenue while awaiting zoning and permit approvals. Todman called for more rental housing near good schools so families could live in those school districts without having to purchase. Green called for incentives to bring in investment and give people more choices about where to live. Shea supported keeping the bipartisan focus on supply and helping families find homes in popular areas.

Memo on More Solutions on the Critical Minerals Problem

Research memo · critical-minerals supply chain and U.S. policy options
Critical mineralsSupply chainsOSINT
Cameron Nichols · May 23, 2025
289 words~1 min read

This document builds on the earlier one by outlining more specific solutions. After doing additional research, I gained a better understanding of how complex and far reaching the critical-minerals supply chain is. Please take a look and let me know which areas you think I should explore further.

China’s control of critical-mineral processing creates a national security risk. A think tank should use open-source intelligence, trade data, corporate records, satellite imagery, and expert interviews to identify vulnerabilities and evaluate U.S. policy options.

  1. Create a Critical-Minerals Risk Monitor
    • Track Chinese processing capacity, export controls, prices, and estimated stockpiles
    • Identify warning signs of supply restrictions
    • Publish regular risk assessments for policymakers and industry
  2. Map China’s Foreign Dependencies
    • Trace lithium imports from Australia, Chile, Argentina, and Africa
    • Map Chinese ownership stakes, contracts, refineries, and shipping routes
    • Identify where allied partnerships could reduce Chinese control
  3. Study Allied Supply-Chain Cooperation
    • Compare the capabilities of Australia, Malaysia, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and European states
    • Assess whether Pax Silica is producing real coordination
    • Recommend ways to share information and prevent competing or duplicate investments
  4. Develop an Investment-Priority Scorecard
    • Rank projects by production potential, cost, completion time, political risk, and exposure to China
    • Examine Lynas’s Malaysian operations and stalled Texas expansion as case studies
    • Identify which projects deserve U.S. financing or purchase guarantees
  5. Model Supply-Disruption Scenarios
    • Simulate Chinese cutoffs of heavy rare earths, gallium, and germanium
    • Test whether Project Vault and allied reserves could sustain key industries
    • Evaluate substitutes such as recycling and Niron’s rare-earth-free magnets

The research would give policymakers and intelligence agencies an unclassified evidence base for anticipating Chinese actions and directing limited resources. The goal would be to identify which policies can reduce Beijing’s leverage without causing unnecessary economic disruption or military escalation.
